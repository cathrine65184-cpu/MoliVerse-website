How Do People Say Hello?

A tour of hellos: handshakes, bows, cheek kisses and a salam.

English, with a little Spanish · Read together with a parent or educator

Before we begin

This is a light issue about greetings. Read it alone or with someone. You can skip any question you like.

A letter to our readers

Hi, children.

Hi, children. Every day you say hello many times: to a teacher, a neighbour, a friend, a shopkeeper. This week we are visiting the Spanish-speaking world, and our first stop is the very first thing we say to each other.

You do not need to know any Spanish to read this. You only need curiosity, and it is fine to read a little at a time.

A word we can explain

A greeting is a small way of saying “I see you.”

A greeting can be words, like hello, or a movement, like a wave. People greet each other almost everywhere, but they do it in different ways. In Spanish you can say hola at any time of day, buenos días in the morning, buenas tardes in the afternoon and buenas noches in the evening.

Buenos días means “good days”, in the plural, as if you were wishing someone a whole bunch of good days at once.

An idea, not a rule

The same hello, in many styles.

In Spain, friends often greet each other with an air kiss next to each cheek, so two kisses. In countries such as Mexico, Argentina and Colombia, one is more usual. In Japan, people may bow. In Thailand, people press their palms together in a wai. In Malaysia, many people shake hands lightly and then touch their own chest, which is often called salam. In New Zealand, Māori people share a hongi, pressing noses together, at special welcomes.

None of these is the “right” way. Each one means roughly “welcome”. If you are not sure what to do, a smile and a hello work almost everywhere.

Think beyond the words

A hello can tell you about people.

Some greetings are close, like hugs. Some keep a little space. Neither is colder or warmer. They come from different habits about what feels polite and comfortable.

You are also allowed to choose how you greet someone. You can wave instead of hugging, and a good friend will understand.

A small word notebook

words or a movement we use to say hello

custom

something a group of people usually does

polite

kind and respectful to others

bow

to bend the top half of the body forward as a greeting

wave

to move your hand to say hello or goodbye

welcome

to be glad someone has arrived

Talk / Wonder / Make

What do you think?

  1. Talk. How do you say hello to your grandparents? Is it different from how you greet a friend?
  2. Wonder. Why do you think some places greet with touch and others with a bow or a wave?
  3. Make. Make up a secret greeting for your family or class. Teach it to someone.

Nothing you say or draw is collected here.

It’s okay to take a break.

If a certain greeting makes you feel shy, that is okay. You can always choose a wave.

For families and educators

Greeting habits differ between families, regions and generations. The examples here are common patterns, not rules. Invite children to describe their own family customs instead of assuming.

Spanish also varies by country (for example tú, vos and usted). This issue uses simple phrases that work widely.
