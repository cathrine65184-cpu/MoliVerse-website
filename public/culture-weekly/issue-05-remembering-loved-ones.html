Flowers, Photos and Favourite Foods: Remembering Loved Ones

How families in different places remember the people they love.

English, with a little Spanish · Read together with a parent or educator

Before we begin

This issue gently talks about remembering people who have died. You can stop at any time, skip any question, or read with a grown-up.

A letter to our readers

Hi, children.

Hi, children. Most families have people they love and miss. This issue is about some of the ways families keep them close.

Nothing here is a test. You can read, think or draw at your own speed.

A word we can explain

To remember means to keep someone close in your heart.

We can remember someone by telling stories, looking at photos, cooking a favourite dish or visiting a special place.

Remembering can be happy, sad, or both at the same time. All of these feelings are normal.

An idea to wonder about

A table full of love.

In Mexico, many families celebrate Día de los Muertos (Day of the Dead) on 1 and 2 November, around the end of October and the beginning of November. Many families build an ofrenda, a special table or altar with photos, candles, flowers such as marigolds, and the favourite foods of the people they remember. UNESCO added this tradition to its list of cultural heritage in 2008.

It is not a scary festival. It is colourful and warm, a way of saying, “We have not forgotten you.”

Think beyond one place

Many places remember in their own way.

In China, many families honour their ancestors at Qingming in spring, tidying family graves and bringing flowers. In Japan, the summer festival of Obon welcomes ancestors with lanterns and dance.

The ways are different, but the idea is similar: love does not stop when someone is gone.

tradition

marigold

Talk / Wonder / Make

What do you think?

  1. Talk. Is there a person or a pet you remember? What did you love about them?
  2. Wonder. Why do you think families use flowers, food and light to remember?
  3. Make. Draw something you would put on a remembrance table, or a happy memory of someone you love.

Nothing you say or draw is collected here.

It’s okay to take a break.

If this issue brings up sad feelings, that is okay. You can take a break and talk to someone you trust.

For families and educators

Please read this first if a child in your care has recently lost someone. Offer to read it together, and let them stop or skip at any point.

Día de los Muertos is a distinct tradition, not a “Mexican Halloween”. UNESCO describes it as an indigenous festivity that blends older pre-Hispanic rites with Catholic traditions. Practices vary by region and family.
