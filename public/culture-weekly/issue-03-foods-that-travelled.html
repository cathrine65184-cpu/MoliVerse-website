Tomatoes, Chocolate and Corn: Foods That Travelled

Where did your favourite foods start their journey?

English, with a little Spanish · Read together with a parent or educator

Before we begin

This issue is about food, so a snack nearby is allowed. Read at your own speed.

A letter to our readers

Hi, children.

Hi, children. Think about your favourite meal. Some of the foods in it may have begun their journey on the other side of the world.

Today’s story is about foods that began in the Americas and later travelled almost everywhere.

A word we can explain

An origin is the place something first comes from.

Tomatoes, potatoes, corn, chocolate, vanilla and chilli peppers were first grown by people in the Americas, long before ships carried them across the ocean.

After about 1492, Spanish and other European ships began carrying these foods to Europe, Africa and Asia. Ships also carried wheat, rice and sugar to the Americas. Historians call this movement of foods, animals and people the Columbian Exchange.

An idea to wonder about

What would pizza be like without tomatoes?

Europe had no tomato sauce until a few hundred years after tomatoes arrived. Chilli peppers also came from the Americas. Before they travelled, many dishes in Asia were spiced in other ways, for example with pepper, ginger or Sichuan peppercorn.

So a dish that feels “very traditional” may be a mix of ingredients from many places. Food has always been on the move.

Think beyond the plate

Words travel with food.

The Spanish word tomate comes from tomatl, a word in Nahuatl, the language of the Aztec people. The English word “tomato” came from it, too. “Chocolate” also came into Spanish from Nahuatl, though experts still discuss exactly how.

The Spanish word maíz (corn) comes from a Taíno word, mahiz. When a food moves, its name often moves with it.

A small word notebook

Open a word when you need it.

origin

the place where something first comes from

ingredient

one of the foods you use to make a dish

journey

a trip from one place to another

trade

buying, selling or swapping things

recipe

instructions for cooking something

exchange

to give one thing and get another

Talk / Wonder / Make

What do you think?

  1. Talk. Which food in your home do you think travelled the farthest?
  2. Wonder. Which foods might not have existed in your country 500 years ago?
  3. Make. Draw a map of one meal. Add arrows showing where each ingredient began.

Nothing you say or draw is collected here.

It’s okay to take a break.

It is okay not to like some foods. Everyone’s tastes are different.

For families and educators

The exchange also brought hardship, including disease and forced labour, which changed many lives. This issue focuses on foods; older children may want to talk about the bigger story with an adult.
